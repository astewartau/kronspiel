Kronspiel

The Rules Commission

Being a study, by simulation, of the Endgame Question — with counsel for the Designer.

✦ ✦ ✦

I.The Question

After the latest rulebook (Bürger reach, throne side-step, stricter Isolation, the uncapturable Krone), play at the table produced two observations:

“Can't do a 2-move rush anymore — now best you can do is a 3-move rush, and there are more counters to it. Last game I did ran 47 turns before I gave up… I think it is actually rather difficult to isolate once the Krone has broken free of starting position.” — the Designer, from the field

Two questions follow. First: is the game broken — can a competent fugitive Krone simply never be caught? Second, and separately: is the game merely slow? And would any of the proposed remedies help without reopening the door to the opening rush?

II.The Four Suspicions

Before measuring anything, the structural case for why a free Krone is hard to catch:

  1. The door arithmetic. The defender needs one open door of eight; the attacker must close the last of them in a single move, because Isolation is judged at the start of the defender's turn and the defender repositions every turn in between.
  2. No forcing moves exist. There is no check. Chess mating nets work because check compels a reply; here the attacker can only herd, never drive.
  3. Zugzwang is a draw. If every defending move would self-isolate, all are illegal — and the Frozen Court rule calls that a draw. The other classic mating tool leaks out through this hatch.
  4. The stricter touch rule cuts both ways. Threats against occupied squares now “count for nothing.” That is what killed the rush — but it also lets a Krone hug his own court more safely in the endgame.

III.The Method

An AI-vs-AI harness plays complete games under variant rules — the shipping game code is never modified; each variant runs against a patched copy of the engine. Both courts are played by the same searcher (iterative-deepening negamax, the game's own AI) with a small randomised tie-break so no two games repeat. Draws by repetition and the fifty-move winter are claimed automatically; games still alive at 180 full moves are recorded as unfinished.

Two regimes:

  • The Courtier's Table — shallow search (depth ≤ 3, 90 ms a move), 80 games per variant. A proxy for casual play, where blunders decide games.
  • The Spymaster's Table — deep search (depth ≤ 5, 350 ms a move), 24 games per variant. A proxy for skilled play, where the fortress problem should live if it exists.

Caveats, honestly stated: 24 deep games per variant is a directional sample, not a tournament — treat single-digit percentage differences as noise. The AI was only taught the Palsied Court rule; it never aims for Hollow Crown wins, and never adopts the shed-and-run strategy that rule exists to punish. And an engine's fortress is not a human's fortress: these results measure the rules' mechanical tendencies, not their feel at a real table.

V.Reading the Results

The headline: the game is not broken — it is slow. Keep the rulebook

Under the current rules, every single deep game ended in Isolation — one hundred percent decisive, no fortress held forever. The fugitive Krone does fall, even against equal, patient defence. What the simulation confirms instead is the pain: the median decisive game ran 41 moves, 42% ran past 50 moves, and the longest ran 178. The 47-turn experience at the table is exactly the median-to-slog band of the current rules. The Endgame Question is a question of pace, not of possibility.

Palsied Court — the surprise of the study Advise against

The armchair case was strong: make zugzwang a weapon and cages convert. The table said otherwise. Once both searches understood the rule, the defenders respected it more than the attackers could exploit it — they kept their Krone scrupulously clear of enemy touch, entrenched early, and the games ballooned: median 90 moves, 79% slogs, and one game in five still unresolved at move 180. Not a single palsy win actually converted. The threat taught the defence fortress-craft and gave the attack nothing it could cash. A rule meant to end sieges instead prolonged them.

Loose Touch, alone — the rush returns Advise against

Reverting Isolation to the old, looser touch rule produced the fastest games of the study (median 28 moves) and full decisiveness — and it also produced a 2-move win at depth 5. The rush is not a novice's trap that strong play refutes; under loose touch it is simply sound. The strict rule is earning its keep, and this measurement is the proof.

Loose + the Truce of Accession — the honourable compromise Worth a human trial

Forbid Isolation before move 6, and the rush dies (one kill in 104 games landed at move 6 exactly — the instant the truce lifts, which argues for setting it a move or two later) while decisiveness stays perfect. But note what did not happen: the hoped-for faster endgame never materialised — with the truce absorbing the early kills, median length landed at 56 moves, indistinguishable from baseline. So this package is a sidegrade, not a cure: the sharper old endgame rule, rush-proofed. If the strict rule ever starts to feel unsatisfying at the table — walls that “should” count but don't — this is the tested alternative. It is not, on this evidence, a pace fix.

Hollow Crown — untested by its nature, cheap by its nature Adopt as insurance

The simulation can neither convict nor acquit this rule, and the reason is instructive: the AI never plays the strategy it punishes. An engine defending on evaluation does not shed its whole court to run — but a losing human does, because under current rules bare-and-running is a genuine path to the Long Winter draw. Hollow Crown closes that path. In 104 games it showed no side effects — lengths and decisive rates tracked baseline within noise. A rule that costs nothing when it is silent and only speaks against the one degenerate strategy is good insurance.

Advancing Winter — right instinct, wrong blade Redesign before trial

As implemented — rings freezing on a fixed schedule, devouring whatever stands on them — the winter truncated games rather than converging them. It claimed roughly eight pieces a game, manufactured empty-court draws (both armies eaten), and pushed decisiveness down to 83–86%, the worst of the study. The instinct is still sound: a closing board is the one mechanism that guarantees an ending. But it should advance only when the game is actually stalling (tie it to the quiet-move clock, not the move number), it should advance slowly, and it should probably drive pieces before it rather than devour them. That version deserves its own study.

A note on the first move

Across the study's 691 decisive games, the Bone Court (moving first) won about 56%. A mild edge, consistent across regimes — worth remembering if tournament play ever matters, and cheaply cured at the table by playing pairs of games with colours swapped.

VI.Counsel

  1. Change nothing in haste. The current rulebook is sound: rush-proof at depth, and every skilled game ends. The strict touch rule, in particular, survived its trial with distinction.
  2. Adopt Hollow Crown when convenient — it costs nothing against honest play and closes the shed-and-run draw against dishonest play. Suggested wording: a Krone whose court is entirely lost falls after his tenth bare turn, unless the enemy court is also bare.
  3. Shelve Palsied Court. The simulation reversed the armchair verdict: it feeds the fortress instead of cracking it.
  4. Keep Loose + Truce in the drawer as the tested fallback if the strict rule ever feels wrong at a human table. Never adopt Loose Touch without the Truce.
  5. If pace still hurts after human play confirms it, the winter is the lever — but rebuilt: advancing on stall, not on schedule. Happy to run that study when the design is settled.
  6. Gather field data. The Chronicle's Copy button exports full game records; a handful of real 40-turn-plus games would show where human conversions stall, which no engine sample can.

VII.Appendix — Reproducing the Study

The harness lives in sim/ beside the game. The shipping code is untouched; variants are generated as patched copies.

node sim/gen-variants.mjs                     # build patched engines
node sim/run.mjs <variant> <games> <seed> [msPerMove] [depth] [label]
node sim/run.mjs baseline 80 1000             # the Courtier's Table
node sim/run.mjs baseline 24 5000 350 5 -deep # the Spymaster's Table
node sim/summarize.mjs                        # the tables above

Variants: baseline, frozenloss (Palsied Court), hollow, smallhammers, loose, loosetruce, winter. Games are seeded and reproducible; results land in sim/out/*.jsonl, one line per game.
