The Question of Flight

Four ways to reconcile Die Flucht with Isolation, weighed in self-play

❖

A flaw came to light in an ordinary game: a Krone hemmed in on every side was spared Isolation by an escape he could not lawfully take. The move Die Flucht forbids leaping through a threatened square; the test for Isolation, in the live game, forgets this — and so credits a flight that never was. Four reconciliations were put to the board and played out by the engine against itself.

Baseline — the live game today — the isolation test credits a Flucht escape the move itself forbids.

Option A — the isolation test demands a legal Flucht (clear, unthreatened path); Flucht can never break a siege.

Option B — the Flucht leap may cross threatened squares — only the landing must be safe; a besieged Krone can flee.

Option C — as B, plus Soft Isolation: a Krone saved only by Flucht is forced to flee next turn (the Crown quits the capitol).

III Counsel

The commission favours Option C.

Baseline is discarded on sight — it is the flaw itself, deciding games on flights that cannot lawfully be flown. Among the three honest reconciliations the board stays decisive under all, and the first-mover balance survives under two — but not the third.

Option B, which lets a Krone vault a besieging ring, hands the Bone Court (who moves first) a marked edge — 57% to 43% against a near-even baseline. The freedom to leap clear of a siege rewards the court that strikes first, and the scales tip.

Option A is the safe hand: perfectly even (50% / 50%) and a touch crisper (median 44 plies vs 47). But it quietly retires Die Flucht as any answer to encirclement — the leap is used scarcely more than today (0.29 vs 0.26 per game). Correct, and a little colourless.

Option C — B with Soft Isolation — keeps Die Flucht a living escape (played 0.48 times a game, near twice the baseline's 0.26), and its forced flight comes into play in roughly one game in 6 (17% of games). Yet because the flight is compelled — spending the once-only leap and surrendering the tempo — it pulls the balance back to near-even (51% / 47%). It preserves the flavour sought — the Crown quitting the capitol for the countryside — without the tilt Option B introduces.

The court that would not fall must run; and having run, has spent its running. — on the Soft Isolation

A caveat of method: the courts here played at a shallow, uniform search, which makes nearly every game decisive — so the draw rates and exact lengths read harsher than a human table would. The balance figures (Bone vs Ash), paired seed for seed, are the firmest signal, and they speak plainly for C.
