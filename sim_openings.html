Kronspiel

The Opening Book

Being a trial of the five named openings of §8 — and the discovery of the Throne Road.

✦ ✦ ✦

I.The Question

The rulebook names five openings, written for Den of Snakes before any engine had ever played the game. The Designer asks: are they good, or period-appropriate folly? What does Kronspiel actually look like in its first moves? And are there other openings worth a name?

II.The Method

Three instruments, all built on the game's own engine:

  • The Oracle — a 90-second deep search scoring every legal move from a position. Scores are in hundredths of a Bürger; +100 is roughly a pawn to the good.
  • Book trials — 24 games per named opening in which the Bone Court is forced to play the scripted line (as long as it stays legal) against a free-thinking opponent, compared with a 48-game free-play control in which Bone won 48%.
  • Field mining and confirmation — the control games' movelists mined for what strong play actually chooses, then the leading candidate first moves re-tested at 20 forced games apiece.

Caveats: 20–24 games resolves the big differences, not the small ones — treat anything within ±10 points of the control as level. And engines are immune to theatre: an opening whose value lives in deceiving a person will test worse here than it plays at a real table. That cuts exactly where you will suspect it cuts.

III.What the Opening Is, in Kronspiel

The Oracle's verdict on the starting position is a study in flatness: 39 legal first moves and barely half a Bürger between the best of them and the worst. This is the rulebook's own §6 made measurable — with every Krone walled in by his own full court, no early attack can touch what matters. Isolation is a mid-game reckoning, and the opening is therefore about position, not tactics.

So Kronspiel has opening principles, not opening theory. The study's sharpest demonstration: taking the strongest first move and then forcing even the Oracle's own preferred second move cost the entire measured advantage (70% → 50%). Scripts die on contact; principles survive. For a game invented for a book about courts and spies, that is a very on-theme property: the memorised line is worth less than the judgment behind it.

The Ashen Approach Sound

Dead level after the book (+9) and dead level in results. The lore says it “wins more often than courtiers like to admit,” and the engine agrees it concedes nothing at all — the only named opening that finishes its script at material and positional par. Tedious to watch, impossible to punish. The Ecclesia would be pleased.

The Closed Gate Sound

The orthodox choice performs exactly as orthodoxy should: slightly passive by the Oracle's count (−87, the cost of spending five tempi on fortification), but the best win rate of the five in practice. A fine default, and a safe thing for a character to play when they wish to reveal nothing — which is, of course, its stated purpose.

The Sigismund Gambit True to its lore

Down nearly two Bürger of value by the end of its own script, winning a third of its games. The rulebook calls it “blunt, costly, and prone to failure against anyone patient enough to let it exhaust itself” — the engine's numbers are that sentence, translated. This is not a flaw in the design; it is characterisation. A soldier's opening should lose to a spymaster.

The Drowned Flank Costly — needs a believer

The worst tested result (25%), and the most instructive failure. The Drowned Flank is a feint, and a feint's value lives entirely in the opponent's belief. An engine does not believe anything; it counts. Against a calculator the wing demonstration is simply five tempi and a loose Marschall. At a human table, against a player who commits their defence to the wrong wing, none of this arithmetic applies — which is precisely why “people who have lost to it” resent it. Keep the lore; just know the trick has a floor.

The Beggar's Gate A true gambit

Genuinely a sacrifice (−250 after book), and the only line the opponent can physically interrupt — in most games the script broke by move 3, the free defender declining to cooperate with the pawn offers. It wins a third of its games anyway, and its games run long: the compensation is real but slow, exactly as §8 describes — the pattern pays off only “by the time it is recognised.” Unsound against precise defence, dangerous against everyone else. That is what a beggar's weapon should be.

V.The Discoveries

The Throne Road — 1. f2–f5 The strongest tested move

The new Bürger triple-dash, played up the one file on the board that runs into the Aschenstuhl. It was the Oracle's top-rated first move, and in forced trials it scored 70% (14–6) with the fastest games of the whole study — a median of 27 moves against 45 for free play. The engine repeatedly found it, and no other first move beat the control by more than noise.

Why this square? Three reasons, in order of subtlety:

  1. The Bürger arrives at the foot of the empty throne, one step from the side-step squares e6 and g6 — the only diagonal walk in the game.
  2. f2 is the one square whose vacancy opens a diagonal for both Kanzler at once. The Oracle's preferred follow-up is immediate: a Kanzler out through the window to e3 or g3. One commoner's march, and both counsellors see daylight.
  3. It concedes nothing: the pawn on f5 is hard to profitably attack, and the Aschenstuhl shields the file beyond it.

Two warnings from the same analysis: taking the side-step at once (f5–e6 before developing) rates poorly — the Road is a development plan, not a pawn raid — and deep Kanzler sorties into enemy territory score negative. The window is for looking through, not leaping through.

The Far Door — the universal reply f10–f7

Against the Throne Road (and nearly everything else), the defence the search converges on is the mirror dash: Ash's f-Bürger to f7, claiming the far side of the throne before the Road's pilgrim can round it. The two central Bürger then stare at each other across the Aschenstuhl — a genuinely distinctive opening picture that no other board game quite produces. Worth naming, because every strong game of Kronspiel will pass through it. And the full dash is load-bearing, as the demonstration below shows — the same crossing Kanzler that wins the Fool's Gate (§VI) arrives, looks at the door, and finds the Krone's escape road open behind it.

The lesser roads

  • e2–e5 and g2–g5 — the flanking dashes, each opening one Prälat's window instead of the double Kanzler window. Tested level with the control (55% and 40%, pooled ≈ par). Honest, slightly quieter siblings of the Road.
  • The wing dashes j2–j5, k2–k4 — the Oracle rates them nearly equal to the Road at the root, but in play they earned exactly nothing (50%). The tempo arrives on a wing where no siege will ever be laid.
  • A trap worth recording: after 1. f2–f5, the Oracle scores Ash's mirror wing dashes (a10–a7, k10–k7) as outright blunders — some 1½ Bürger worse than the Far Door. The one early mistake the opening allows is answering the centre with the edge.

Die Flucht is half the game

In the free-play control, a Krone spent Die Flucht in 24 of 48 games. The once-per-game escape is not a curiosity; it is a live resource in every second game, and its mere availability shapes the opening — every early Krone move permanently spends it. A finding worth a sentence in §7: good players treat the unspent Flucht as a piece in hand.

VI.The Second Expedition

Two follow-up hunts, 160 further games and five further oracle positions: does the Far Door have a rival, and is the early Flucht a plan or a sin? The first hunt found something better than a rival.

The Fool's Gate — a forced win in two moves A trap, verified

1. f2–f5  f10–f9??  2. e1–j6 — and the Ash Krone is isolated on move two. The engine confirms it cold: every neighbour of f11 is his own court, the one empty door is f10, and the Kanzler on j6 stares at that door from clear across the board, down the diagonal the first move opened. The mirror 2. g1–b6 works identically.

The mechanism is the beautiful part. The losing move looks natural — it steps toward the centre, it was the most popular reply among shallow engines (and killed seven of them in twenty games), and even a 90-second oracle one ply earlier rated it level with the best. Its sin is invisible to material counting: the half-step pawn stands on his own Krone's Flucht corridor. Dash fully to f7 and f9 stays empty — the Krone's untouched Flucht counts as an open door, and the trap evaporates. Stop one square short, and your own commoner has bolted the king's road behind him.

Every folk game earns its cautionary miniature — this is Kronspiel's, and it teaches the game's two deepest lessons in a single stroke: your own court is the wall that kills you, and the unspent Flucht is a piece in hand.

The Far Door has no rival — only two forbidden answers

Six forced Ash replies to the Throne Road, twenty games each: f10–f7 and the flanking doors e10–e7, g10–g7, e10–e8 all land in a statistical dead heat in play (Bone 50–65%, inside the noise of these samples). The oracle separates them where the trials cannot: Bone's best continuation is worth about 265 against the Far Door and about 310 against every flanking door — a real but modest edge to f10–f7, roughly half a Bürger. So the Far Door keeps its title on points, the flanks are honourable seconds, and two answers are now confirmed forbidden by both trial and oracle: the edge dash a10–a7 loses ground (Bone 70%), and f10–f9 loses the game. The distilled rule for §8: on the f-file, dash fully or not at all; and never answer the centre with the edge.

The Flucht question: procession or hoard?

Interventional test — the Throne Road played with and without the Krone's early Flucht-procession (4. f1–f4): 79% against 75%. No measurable cost either way; the grand early Flucht is a genuine freedom, not an error. The observational data leans the other way at the far end of the game, though: across all 656 recorded side-games, a Krone who kept his Flucht and finally spent it late won 55%, while one who had already walked it away won 42%. Confounded numbers (besieged Krones are forced to walk) — but together with the trap above, the counsel writes itself: the Flucht is worth most as a held threat, and worth everything as an open door.

VII.Counsel for the Designer

  1. The five names survive contact with the engine — as literature and as design. The two “respectable” openings test sound; the three disreputable ones test unsound in exactly the manner their own descriptions promise. A rulebook whose flavour text is quantitatively honest is a rare and pleasing thing. Change nothing.
  2. Consider adding the Throne Road and the Far Door to §8 as the modern main line — the opening a Kaltmann or a Sigismund-trained player would know cold. Suggested register: “The Throne Road — the commoner's march to the foot of the empty chair, favoured since the reach rule entered the rulebook. Its refutation has not been found; its imitators on the wings have found nothing at all.”
  3. Let the fiction exploit the Drowned Flank's true nature: it is the one opening whose strength depends on the opponent's credulity — a purpose-built scene where a character wins with an objectively bad opening, or loses by trusting one.
  4. Opening principles for the primer, if wanted: dash a centre Bürger; open a counsellor's window and use it modestly; meet the dash at the Far Door; on the f-file, dash fully or not at all; never answer the centre with the edge; and spend Die Flucht like gold, because half of all games are decided with it in hand or in flight.
  5. Give the Fool's Gate to the book. A folk game with a named two-move trap is a folk game with teeth, and this one demonstrates the whole philosophy of Kronspiel in three plies. Suggested register for §8: “The Fool's Gate — the provincial's funeral: 1. f2–f5 f10–f9 2. e1–j6, and the Krone is walled alive by his own commoner. Every child of the capital is shown it once; the ones worth teaching never need it shown twice.”

VIII.Appendix — Reproducing the Study

node sim/gen-variants.mjs                          # build the analysis engine
node sim/openings.mjs analyze [moves…]             # the Oracle (e.g. analyze f2-f5)
node sim/openings.mjs book <opening> 24 11000      # closedgate | sigismund | ashen | drowned | beggars
node sim/openings.mjs free 24 12000 -a             # free-play control
node sim/openings.mjs first f2-f5 20 13000         # forced first move
node sim/openings.mjs line name "f2-f5,e1-g3" 20   # scripted line (Bone only)
node sim/openings.mjs duo name "f2-f5,f10-f9" 20   # scripted prefix, both sides

Every game is seeded and reproducible; movelists are recorded in full in sim/out/op-*.jsonl.

The steppable diagrams on this page are <kron-demo> elements — a small reusable framework in js/demo.js. Give it a move list and optional per-ply notes, and it replays the line through the game's real engine (captures, dashes, side-steps, Die Flucht and promotion all behave exactly as at the table), rendered with the Court Sigils.
